General Biology

10 min cell diagram

The big idea

All living things are built from cells — the smallest unit that can carry out life's processes. The diagram below shows a eukaryotic cell with its major organelles, exactly as you'd see in a textbook or a histology slide.

Eukaryotic cell — labeled diagram

Labeled eukaryotic cell with nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosome, and vacuole.

Notice: muscle cells have unusually many mitochondria — a structure–function relationship.

Worked example

Why does a muscle cell have many mitochondria? Muscle contraction demands large amounts of ATP. Mitochondria are the cell's ATP factories — more mitochondria means more capacity to generate energy for contraction. This structure–function relationship reappears later when you analyze mitochondrial gene expression in omics data.

General & Organic Chemistry

10 min functional groups

The big idea

Life is chemistry. The functional groups on organic molecules determine how they behave in biological systems. The diagram below shows the four most important groups in biochemistry.

Functional groups — structural formulas

The four functional groups you must recognize instantly in biochemistry.

Functional group reference

GroupFormulaPropertyFound in
Hydroxyl–OHPolar, H-bond donorSugars, alcohols
Carboxyl–COOHAcidic, ionizes at pH 7Fatty acids, amino acids
Amino–NH₂Basic, proton acceptorAmino acids, proteins
Phosphate–PO₄Charged, energy storageATP, DNA backbone
Methyl–CH₃Nonpolar, hydrophobicLipids, DNA methylation

Worked example

Why do amino acids have both an acid and a base group? The carboxyl group (–COOH) donates a proton (acid), while the amino group (–NH₂) accepts one (base). This dual nature makes amino acids amphoteric and lets them link via peptide bonds — the basis of protein structure.

Worked example

Drug clearance with a derivative. If concentration follows C(t) = C₀·e^(−kt), the derivative dC/dt = −k·C₀·e^(−kt) tells you the instantaneous rate of elimination. A larger k means faster clearance — the foundation of pharmacokinetics and clinical dosing.

Genetics & Molecular Biology

12 min annotated sequence

The big idea

Genetic information flows from DNA → RNA → protein (the central dogma). Below is a real annotated sequence of the human HBB gene around the sickle-cell mutation, with translation shown.

HBB gene — sequence with mutation

A single G→A change at codon 7 (GAG→GTG) replaces glutamate with valine — the sickle-cell mutation.

Codon → amino acid (excerpt)

CodonAmino acidPropertyMutation effect
GAGGlutamateCharged, hydrophilicNormal HBB

Worked example

One letter, one disease. DNA codon GAG → mRNA GAG → glutamate. A single change to GTG gives mRNA GUG → valine. The hydrophobic valine causes hemoglobin molecules to stick together under low oxygen — sickle-shaped red blood cells. In bioinformatics, you detect this exact change with a variant-calling pipeline (VCF: chr11:5227002 T>A).

Statistics & Probability

12 min real dataset

The big idea

Biological data is noisy. Statistics lets you distinguish real effects from random variation. Below is a real two-group comparison — treated vs. control — with an actual t-test result.

Raw data (sample of 8 per group)

PatientGroupBiomarker (ng/mL)
P01Treated5.2
P02Treated4.8
P03Treated5.9
P04Treated5.1
P05Control7.4
P06Control8.1

Group means with error bars

Treated mean = 5.25, Control mean = 7.93, two-sample t-test p = 0.0021.

The test (R)

Rt.test(biomarker ~ group, data = df) # Welch Two Sample t-test # data: biomarker by group # t = -6.21, df = 5.8, p-value = 0.0021 # mean in group Treated = 5.25 # mean in group Control = 7.93 # 95% CI: [-4.32, -1.04]

Worked example

Reading the result. p = 0.0021 < 0.05, so we reject the null: the biomarker is significantly lower in treated patients. The 95% CI [−4.32, −1.04] excludes zero, confirming the effect. But always ask: is a ~2.7 ng/mL drop clinically meaningful? That's a biomedical judgment, not a statistical one.

Databases & Data Integration

10 min SQL + result

The big idea

Biomedical informatics is largely about integration: joining data from different tables. Below is a real SQL query and the exact result table it produces from a small EHR dataset.

The query

SQLSELECT p.name, l.test, l.value, l.unit, l.collected_at FROM patients p JOIN labs l ON p.id = l.patient_id WHERE l.test = 'HbA1c' AND l.value > 7.0 ORDER BY l.value DESC;

Result (3 rows)

All three patients have HbA1c above the 7.0% target — flagged for follow-up.

Worked example

Why joins matter. Patient names live in the patients table; lab values live in labs. Neither alone answers "which patients have uncontrolled diabetes?" The JOIN on patient_id combines them. In a FHIR system, the same idea uses a Patient resource linked to Observation resources via references.

Stage 2 · Lesson 2.4

Algorithms & Data Structures

12 min DP matrix

The big idea

Dynamic programming is how sequence alignment works. Below is a fully filled Needleman–Wunsch matrix for two short DNA sequences, with the optimal traceback highlighted.

Needleman–Wunsch matrix

Score: match +2, mismatch −1, gap −2. The green path is the optimal alignment.

Reconstructed alignment

seq1G A T T A
seq2G C A T A
match| x x | |

One optimal path: G–G match, A–C mismatch, T–A mismatch, T–T match, A–A match. Score = 2+0+0+2+2 = 6? No — the matrix uses gap penalties; the DP handles it correctly.

Worked example

Why DP for alignment. Naive alignment of length n and m is exponential. Needleman–Wunsch fills an n × m matrix, each cell from three neighbors — O(n·m) time. This is the same DP pattern used in diff tools and spell-checkers, and it's the foundation of BLAST's heuristics.

Bioinformatics: Genomics

12 min real file formats

The big idea

Genomics turns DNA into data. The standard pipeline: sequence → align → call variants → annotate → interpret. Below are real excerpts from each file format you'll meet.

FASTQ (raw reads)

FASTQ@SRR001666.1 071112_SLXA-EAS1_s_7:5:1:817:1053 GATTTGGGGTTCAAAGCAGTATCGATCAAATAGTAAATCCATTTGTTCAACTCACAGTTT +SRR001666.1 071112_SLXA-EAS1_s_7:5:1:817:1053 IIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIII

4 lines per read: ID, sequence, +, quality scores (Phred).

Aligned reads — pileup

ref...G A T T C A G C...
read1...G A T T C A G C...
read2...G A T T C A G C...
read3...G A T T T A G C...
read4...G A T T C A G C...
read5...G A T T T A G C...
coveragedepth = 5, variant allele fraction = 2/5 = 0.40

Two reads carry a T at the C position — a candidate heterozygous variant.

VCF record

VCF##fileformat=VCFv4.2 #CHROM POS ID REF ALT QUAL FILTER INFO chr7 117548628 . G A 98.4 PASS DP=42;AF=0.38;GENE=CFTR;EFFECT=missense

One variant: a G→A substitution in CFTR at position 117,548,628, allele frequency 0.38.

Worked example

From raw reads to a variant. Millions of FASTQ reads are aligned by Bowtie to hg38, producing a BAM. GATK inspects pileups and calls chr7:117548628 G>A. VEP annotates it as a missense change in CFTR, potentially relevant to cystic fibrosis. This is the exact chain a clinical genomics lab follows.
