The big idea

Worked example

Why does a muscle cell have many mitochondria? Muscle contraction demands large amounts of ATP. Mitochondria are the cell's ATP factories — more mitochondria means more capacity to generate energy. This structure–function relationship reappears in omics data.

General & Organic Chemistry

10 min functional groups

The big idea

Life is chemistry. The functional groups on organic molecules determine how they behave in biological systems.

Functional group reference

Why do amino acids have both an acid and a base group? The carboxyl group (–COOH) donates a proton (acid), while the amino group (–NH₂) accepts one (base). This dual nature lets them link via peptide bonds.

12 min annotated sequence

Genetic information flows from DNA → RNA → protein. Below is a real annotated sequence of the human HBB gene around the sickle-cell mutation.

Algorithms & Data Structures

12 min DP matrix

The big idea

Dynamic programming is how sequence alignment works. Below is a filled Needleman–Wunsch matrix.

Needleman–Wunsch matrix

Score: match +2, mismatch −1, gap −2.

Worked example

Why DP for alignment. Naive alignment is exponential. Needleman–Wunsch fills an n × m matrix, each cell from three neighbors — O(n·m) time. This is the same DP pattern used in diff tools and spell-checkers.

What you're doing

Realistic mockup: protein-interaction network
BFS from A: B,C (dist 1) → D (dist 2) → E,F (dist 3). DFS from A: A→B→D→E→F→C.

Your workflow steps

  • Define the task: "find shortest paths from A" → BFS; "check connectivity/cycles" → DFS.
  • Choose the structure: adjacency list (sparse graph, O(V+E) memory).
  • Trace BFS: queue = [A] → visit B,C → visit D → visit E,F. Order: A,B,C,D,E,F.
  • Justify: BFS O(V+E) gives shortest paths in unweighted graphs.
